Case study · Product concept · 2026

Nudge — a learning companion built for the day after you miss a day.

Existing platforms host courses. Nudge helps people keep going with them — across YouTube, Coursera, Udemy and anything with a link.

Role
Research, product strategy, UX, UI, design system
Timeline
2026 · self-initiated
Tools
Figma
Platform
Mobile (iOS/Android concept)
Context

Why this, why now

Most of my own learning — and my friends' — happens on YouTube, not in a walled course platform. That's convenient and completely unstructured. People start with real intent, then a busy week happens, and the course just… stops.

Nudge doesn't host content. It sits alongside whatever you're already using and does the one thing platforms don't: helps you build a habit and recover when it breaks.

Problem

Motivation isn't the problem. Recovery is.

People who start learning online begin with strong motivation but struggle to stay consistent. Platforms provide content but rarely help users build sustainable habits — or return after an interruption.

What made it hard: every existing pattern (streaks, reminders, progress bars) is designed for the person who's already showing up. The moment someone misses two days, those same patterns turn into guilt — and guilt is what makes people avoid the app entirely.

Research

What 15 learners told me

I interviewed 15 people across four groups — students, working professionals, career switchers and lifelong learners — with one goal: understand why people abandon online courses despite wanting to continue.

  • 53%

    said consistency was their biggest challenge

  • 87%

    used YouTube as their primary learning source

  • 15

    participants across 4 learner types

  1. 01Lack of consistency — the course drops out of the routine after one busy week.
  2. 02Too many courses, no structure — six started, none finished.
  3. 03Busy, irregular schedules — study happens in the evening, if at all.
  4. 04Guilt after missing days — which makes reopening the app feel worse.
  5. 05No visible progress — a 15% bar on a 40-hour course isn't motivating.
Most participants didn't quit intentionally. They simply stopped showing up after missing a few days.
Strategy

How might we help learners return after missing a day — without guilt or overwhelm?

Instead of trying to be a better course platform, Nudge would be a companion: bring your own content, and we'll handle the habit. That reframing set three design principles I checked every screen against.

  • Simple

    Clean layouts, one clear next action, no distraction. The app should take less than a minute a day to use.

  • Encouraging

    Positive tone, small wins made visible. Progress in minutes and confidence, not just percent complete.

  • Non-judgmental

    Meet learners where they are. No shame, no comparison, no red broken streak.

Design process

From persona to flows

The primary persona, Arjun, is a second-year B.Tech student in Nagpur who learns web development in the evenings on a phone, has started 6+ courses and finished none. His goal isn't "learn CSS" — it's build one real project he can show an interviewer.

That reframed onboarding. Instead of asking "what do you want to learn?", Nudge asks for the main goal, a realistic daily time budget (5 minutes is a valid answer), and a link to the first course. Three steps, under a minute.

Key decisions

Four decisions that made it Nudge

Decision 01

Recovery mode instead of a broken streak

Miss two days and the app says "That's okay. Your progress is still here," then offers a 5-minute catch-up or "remind me tonight."

Why: the research said people quit at the guilt step, not the motivation step. Removing the punishment removes the reason to avoid opening the app.

Decision 02

Bring your own content

Paste a YouTube playlist or a Coursera link. Nudge turns it into a lesson list and opens the source when you're ready to watch.

Why: 87% of participants already learn on YouTube. Competing with that is a losing strategy; wrapping it is not.

Decision 03

Sessions measured in minutes, not modules

Daily goals start at 5 minutes. A lesson ends with one quick check and a small XP reward for showing up.

Why: irregular schedules were a top pain point. A goal you can hit on a bad day is the only goal that builds a habit.

Decision 04

Show growth, not just completion

The progress screen tracks lessons done, time spent, skills unlocked and a self-rated confidence score before vs now.

Why: "no visible progress" was a pain point. Confidence moving from 3.3 to 6.3 is a story a percent bar can't tell.

Design system

Calm by default

A restrained system: one primary green for trust and focus, one secondary green for energy and progress, a neutral grey scale, and Inter throughout for readability. Everything on screen had to feel like a companion, not a dashboard.

Final design

The full flow

Thirteen screens covering onboarding, daily learning, recovery, growth, peers and an AI doubt-support feature that answers a question and points back to the related lesson.

Outcome

What it's designed to move

Nudge is a concept, so these are the targets the design is built around — not measured results. They're the numbers I'd instrument first if this shipped.

  • 78%

    of users return after missing a day

  • 2.6×

    better consistency with the recovery-first approach

  • 65%

    of users complete more lessons

Reflection

What I learned

People don't quit learning because they lack ambition. They quit because staying consistent is difficult — and most products make the comeback harder than the start.

Designing for recovery rather than perfection changed every screen, including the ones that had nothing to do with missing days. If I took this further I'd prototype the recovery flow first, test it with the same 15 people, and only then touch the visual system.

Small steps. Consistent action. Real progress. That's the Nudge way — and, honestly, it's how I try to work too.
